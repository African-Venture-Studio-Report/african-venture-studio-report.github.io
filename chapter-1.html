How does the venture studio model affect access to first external capital for startups in Africa, and what can investors, studio operators and development finance institutions learn from it?

The case for studios rests on a young evidence base built on specific groups of companies

Development finance institutions, foundations and commercial investors now back African venture studios to fill the “missing middle” between idea and growth capital. The most comprehensive study of African studios, published by FMO, GIZ SAIS and Briter in April 2026, finds that half of the studio-built companies it tracks raise follow-on funding after initial studio support, against around a third of accelerator companies and a fifth of incubator companies. Studio companies also reach pre-seed and seed funding about a year sooner, and 90 percent of studio-built companies raise their follow-on funding as equity or debt.1

Studies outside Africa, on self-reported or differently built samples, point the same way. The Global Startup Studio Network reports that 72 percent of seed funded studio companies reach Series A, against 42 percent of conventional startups, and Max Pog finds that studio companies raise a seed round in a median 1.49 years, against 3.03. The Venture Studio Index reports no funding rates.2 Academic work on studios is recent and qualitative. Case studies describe the studio as a distinct type of organisation that produces “validated, investment-ready ventures”, and stress how widely studio designs vary.3

The studies reviewed here do not measure the funding rate across entire disclosed studio portfolios. Briter’s benchmark measures capital raised after initial studio support, including grants, debt and blended finance. Its population is the ventures studios built in-house, drawn from Briter Intelligence records of disclosed companies, and the white paper notes that “some under-reporting may exist for companies that dissolved before raising funds.”4 Disclosed portfolios list every company a studio holds, whether or not it has raised, so the funding rate across them can be measured on an independent deal record, Africa: The Big Deal (Exhibit 1).

Disclosed portfolios make a whole-portfolio rate measurable

The study covers 68 verified African venture studios. Forty-nine disclose portfolios containing 456 unique companies. It makes four contributions to the published record:

  • A funding rate for all 456 portfolio companies on an independent deal record, compared round by round with Briter’s benchmark.

  • A comparison with startups outside studios on the same deal record, sources, thresholds and dates.

  • An investor-by-investor map of who writes the first external cheque, who follows and where African investors fall away.

  • A directory of 68 verified studios, reconciled with Briter’s and adding 27 not yet in its publications.

The first external round can give a studio’s stake its first outside price

A priced equity round gives the studio’s stake its first outside reference value and ends the company’s sole reliance on studio funding. It neither sells the studio’s shares nor provides liquidity, and studio support often continues beyond it (Chapter 7). An exit or a sale of shares in a later round can then realise that value, which is mainly how a studio recycles its capital (Exhibit 2). The first recorded external round of a company that raised before the deal record opens in 2019 may not be its first outside price.

Capital recycling matters more as private funds return less cash to investors and development finance commits a smaller share to Africa-focused venture funds

The FMO, GIZ SAIS and Briter white paper lists capital and liquidity among the structural constraints on studios. Studios carry higher operating costs than venture funds and cannot live on management fees, so they depend on “earlier and more frequent liquidity events”.5 In McKinsey’s January 2026 survey of 300 limited partners, distributions to paid-in capital rank joint second among allocation metrics, level with multiple of invested capital. Globally, private equity distributions fell to 6 percent of assets under management in the year to June 2025, against an average of 16 percent from 2015 to 2019.6 Development finance institutions, the anchor investors in African venture, provided about 45 percent of commitments to Africa-focused venture funds in 2022 to 2024 and 27 percent in 2025 (Exhibit 3).7 A studio’s ability to recycle capital therefore depends on its companies reaching the financing milestones that make exits and share sales possible, and the first external round is the earliest of them.

Published benchmarks track follow-on funding among companies that have already raised. The share of all portfolio companies that reach a first external round has yet to be measured, and it matters more now that DFIs and LPs need studio capital to recycle sooner. Chapter 2 sets out how this report measures it across 68 studios.
