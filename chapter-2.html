Sixty-eight verified studios extend Briter’s landscape

Briter’s 2026 white paper anchors the definition, the directory and the benchmarks

The white paper is the most comprehensive public source on African venture studios and the reference point for this report. Briter Intelligence funding records for more than 3,500 African startups from 2015 to 2025 underpin it.8 The white paper Venture Studios in Africa, published with FMO and GIZ SAIS in April 2026, counts “40+ active studios” and profiles 41 studios across 13 countries, with seven profiles validated by the studios themselves and 34 compiled from public sources.9 The African Venture Studio Summit Directory 2026 profiles 17 studios and restates the white paper’s definition.10

We adopt the white paper’s definition, build our directory from its annex and test our findings against its benchmarks. Its authors describe it as “a structured contribution to an evolving discussion” and present its counts as minimums.11 The 27 studios added by this study, not yet in Briter’s publications, extend that discussion.

All 68 studios meet the five published criteria, and three further conditions set the sample’s scope

All 68 studios meet the five published criteria. Each builds companies to investment readiness, recruits co-founders to run them, provides an in-house operating team, invests its own cash and takes equity in return. Three conditions of our own set the sample’s scope and observability (Exhibit 4). They do not change which organisations count as studios. Condition 6 records how many ventures each studio discloses, and its three-venture threshold is tracked for transparency without being a condition of entry. Condition 7 requires studios with a disclosed portfolio to have built a venture by the end of 2023, which gives each portfolio an earlier start for observing funding progression, although the second-round analysis applies its own first round cohort and 24-month window. Condition 8 admits a venture-building arm inside a fund, corporation or foundation when it meets the five criteria. The sample covers studios headquartered or mainly operating in Africa and active in October 2026, including one fully remote pan-African studio. Accelerators, incubators, agencies and organisations that have closed or changed model are excluded and listed in Annex D.

Each studio’s structure is classified from studio websites, published reports, regional technology press, company registries, Crunchbase and PitchBook. Briter’s classification is kept for the 41 studios it lists unless these sources clearly show a different structure. The 27 studios added by this study carry the main risk of misclassification, because whether they invest their own cash and take equity is mostly inferred from these sources. Practitioner interviews informed the approach but were not used to classify any studio.

Portfolio disclosure determines which studios’ funding outcomes can be measured

Every funding rate in the analysis rests on the 49 studios that disclose a portfolio. The study assessed 89 organisations, retained 68 verified studios and found portfolios at 49 of them, which together hold 456 companies (Exhibit 5). The 68 verified studios are concentrated in Nigeria, South Africa and Kenya, home to 40 of them (Exhibit 6).

The larger sample reflects wider coverage under the same definition

Exhibit 7 reconciles the two counts studio by studio. Two of the seven Briter studios outside the sample were excluded on review, one reclassified and one too recently established. The other five appear only in the Summit Directory and have not yet been assessed against the five criteria.

The 27 additions come from a country-by-country search of studio disclosures and regional press, and each meets the same five criteria.

Five studios hold 30 percent of portfolio entries, and North Africa has the thinnest disclosure

Five studios hold 30 percent of all portfolio entries, which gives their companies a disproportionate influence on comparisons made on entries, though less on tests run with the studio as the unit. About seven in ten studios publish a portfolio, a share that varies little with Briter listing or structure (Exhibit 8). Regional coverage is uneven. Six of North Africa’s 11 studios publish one, against 13 of Southern Africa’s 15, so North African results rest on the narrowest evidence. Central Africa enters through one studio in Angola, grouped with Southern Africa, and interview evidence is limited to studio practitioners. Exhibit 42 sets out how each limitation moves the findings.

Comparisons between studio groups count every portfolio entry

Comparisons between groups of studios rest on the 464 portfolio entries, because eight companies sit in two studios’ portfolios and each studio is credited with every company it lists. All eight have raised, so the funding rate on entries is 28.4 percent, against 27.2 percent on companies. These eight create duplication across portfolios. A separate issue is that entries at the same studio share its practices and are not statistically independent, so Chapter 5 also tests its structural findings with the studio as the unit. Exhibit 9 defines all seven measurement units.

Structure findings are associations, because portfolios do not show which companies a studio built and which it backed

Each studio is classified by how it funds its companies, using the three structures in FMO, GIZ SAIS and Briter (2026). A single entity studio builds and invests from one balance sheet (38 studios). A dual entity studio pairs the studio with an internally managed fund (10) or a separately governed sidecar fund (1). The ten with an internal fund are referred to throughout as studios with their own funds. Nine hybrid studios combine venture building with fee-based services or accelerator programmes, two operate within corporations and one is donor backed. Seven, with 35 portfolio entries, could not be classified from public evidence.

Public evidence does not consistently establish whether each studio built, co-created or invested in each company (the build route), so structural findings remain associations. Briter’s benchmark measures studio-built companies, while studios with their own funds also invest in companies they did not build. Recording the build route, the first open question in Chapter 9, would make the two measures directly comparable.

Studios with their own funds are 10 of the 68 studios but hold 72 of the 132 funded entries, more than half, including companies they may have backed without building (Exhibit 10). The median studio with a portfolio lists eight companies, and four list more than 20.

The 68 studios build on Briter’s published landscape, though only studios with a public footprint could be included. The 49 that publish a portfolio are the base for every rate in this report, and Chapter 3 measures how many of their 456 companies reach a first external round.
