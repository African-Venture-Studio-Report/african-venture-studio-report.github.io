Chapter Three

The funding path narrows most sharply at the first external round

In this report

Executive summary1 · Published benchmarks cover narrower groups than whole studio portfolios2 · Sixty-eight verified studios extend Briter’s landscape3 · The funding path narrows most sharply at the first external round4 · The investor pipeline is thin, fragmented and mostly non-African by Series B5 · Studios’ own funds are the strongest fundability marker6 · The studio advantage appears after the first external round7 · Studio support tapers as companies mature, with stalled rounds still to be explained8 · Make first rounds measurable, then pilot what lifts them9 · Evidence on build routes and studio stakes would test the report’s central findingsTechnical annexReferencesForeword and notice

The first external round is where most studio companies fall away. Fewer than three in ten have one on record. Those that do tend to keep raising, with about three in five reaching each later round, and funded studio companies raise a second round within 24 months more often than other startups. Most recorded capital goes to a few late rounds, while the largest loss of companies comes before any outside investor arrives.

27%of 456 disclosed portfolio companies have a recorded external round
60%of funded companies at each stage, on average, go on to raise the next round
48% vs 28%of funded companies raise a second round within 24 months, studio against other startups

Africa: The Big Deal records at least one external round for 124 of the 456 portfolio companies, a recorded funding rate of 27.2 percent. Among companies with a recorded first external round, progression is steadier, with 70 raising at least two rounds, 45 at least three and 28 at least four, so about 60 percent reach the next round at each step (Exhibit 11).

The headline rate holds under alternative definitions of an external round, staying between 26.3 and 28.9 percent (Exhibit 12). The two widest swings, counting studio-only rounds as external and treating two studio-linked investors as part of that studio, each move eight companies or fewer and alter no comparison in the report.

Portfolio funding rates converge with the benchmark after the first round

Measured the same way as Briter’s benchmark, 27.2 percent of portfolio companies have a recorded first external round, 15.4 percent a second and 9.9 percent a third, against 40, 22 and 9 percent in the FMO-hosted edition of the white paper and 49, 28 and 14 percent in the second edition (Exhibit 13). 12 The gap is widest at the first round, at 12.8 to 21.8 percentage points, and closes by the third, where portfolio companies range from 0.9 percentage points ahead to 4.1 behind. Once companies have raised, the rates align: 56 percent of companies here reach a second round after a first, against 55 and 57 percent in the two editions. The difference lies in reaching the first round.

The gap compares two different measures, so it does not indicate underperformance. They differ in population, build route, data source and period. Two of these differences may lift this report’s rate. Portfolios include companies studios backed, which may raise more readily than those they built, and failed ventures may have dropped off studio websites. The deal record may lower it, as it omits rounds below $100,000, and below higher thresholds before 2021, so some small first rounds that Briter records may be missing here. Any record built from disclosures can also miss companies that closed early, as the white paper notes. None of these effects can be measured, so part of the first-round gap may reflect differences in what each source records.

Once funded, studio companies raise a second round more often than other startups

Funded studio companies raise a second round more often than other funded startups. Among 80 studio companies whose first recorded round closed between January 2021 and September 2024, 48 percent raised again within 24 months, against 28 percent of 1,612 startups with no recorded connection to the 68 studios (Exhibit 14). Allowing for region and first round year, their odds of a second round are 2.21 times as high (95% confidence interval 1.39 to 3.52).13 The difference stays significant at the 5 percent level when companies are clustered by studio (p = 0.006), but not after correction for multiple testing (Exhibit 40). The 28 percent for other startups is consistent with the white paper’s finding that nearly 70 percent of African ventures do not secure follow-on funding after their first round.14

The comparison is confined to companies that have already raised. No dataset records startups that never raised or that closed, and launch years exist only for funded companies, so entry, survival and time to first funding cannot be compared. Funded studio companies raise again more often, and whether studio support drives that advantage is the question Chapter 9 sets out to test.

The advantage holds for early-stage first rounds, companies launched since 2019 and companies in the same sector, so later entry or faster-moving sectors are unlikely to explain it (Exhibit 14). It is concentrated in studios with their own funds, whose companies raised again at 69 percent, against 29 percent at single entity studios, close to the 28 percent for other startups (Exhibit 15). It also leans on the largest portfolio, which holds 21 of the 80 cohort companies and whose fund backs companies the studio did not build. Excluding it lowers the studio rate to 39 percent and the odds ratio to 1.60 (95% confidence interval 0.93 to 2.77, unclustered p = 0.12), which is no longer significant.

Studio selection may account for part of the advantage

Studios filter their portfolios before any investor sees them. Those in the white paper report dropping about 80 percent of ideas before operation, and 15 to 20 percent of operating ventures still fail. 15 The cohorts also start from different points. Studio companies are more often West African (44 percent against 35 percent of other startups), more often began with a pre-seed or seed round (50 percent against 29 percent) and less often with a grant (6 percent against 18 percent).

The comparison controls for region and stage, but not for what a studio sees before it builds.

Accelerator research separates selection from support only where admission was by lottery or score cutoff. In Start-Up Chile, cash and workspace alone made no measurable difference to later fundraising, while structured training did.16 The Global Accelerator Learning Initiative, which follows more than 4,210 accelerated and rejected ventures, finds that accelerated ventures grow faster, though those in emerging markets still attract less equity and debt.17 No African studio dataset yet supports such a design, so the report does not attribute the difference to studio support. Studios that record the ventures they considered and declined would make the test possible.

Funded studio companies launched since 2019 took a median of two years to reach a first external round

Launch years are recorded only for companies with funding records, so the two-year median, based on 78 companies, describes those that reached investors and may not represent the wider portfolio. Funded companies that raise again do so a median of 13 months after their first external round, and half of the 102 with at least 24 months of record have a second round within 24 months (Exhibit 16). This exceeds the 48 percent for the 2021 to 2024 cohort because it includes first rounds from 2019 and 2020.

More than half of funded studio companies have no recorded round in the past two years

Sixty-four of the 124 funded companies (52%) have recorded no external round in the 24 months to September 2026, including two thirds of those that raised only once (36 of 54). Excluding the three acquired companies, the share is 50 percent (61 of 121). Recorded dormancy is lower among companies of studios with their own funds than among those of single entity studios, at 43 and 60 percent, but the difference is not statistically significant (p = 0.14, Exhibit 17). In the follow-on comparison cohort defined earlier, 64 percent of studio companies have recorded no round since September 2024, against 84 percent of other startups.

The pipeline therefore thins after entry as well as at it. One possible reason, examined in Chapter 4, is that the investors who write early cheques rarely reappear in later rounds.

Capital is concentrated in a few companies, late rounds and debt

Capital is concentrated in a few companies, so totals overstate the typical outcome and the analysis rests on rates and medians. Moove and Flutterwave account for 50.2 percent of the $2.45 billion traced into the 124 funded companies, and five companies for 64.0 percent. The median funded company has raised $2.0 million in total, in rounds with a median of $1.0 million. Studio companies also enter with larger first rounds, at a median of $500,000 against $200,000 for other startups in the comparison cohort. Recorded rounds peaked at 62 in 2022 and fell to 44 in 2025.

Studio companies are 4.9 percent of funded African companies in the deal record but take 7.1 percent of rounds and 10.9 percent of capital, more than twice their share by number, though two companies account for half of that capital (Exhibit 18). They form a small but capital-intensive part of a market in which almost 500 startups raised rounds of $100,000 or more in 2025 alone.18

Most capital arrives after the first cheque. The 127 grant, pre-seed and seed rounds carry $180 million, 7.4 percent of the capital traced, while 13 rounds at Series B and later, raised by eight companies, carry $1.13 billion, or 46 percent (Exhibit 19).

Debt accounts for a fifth of the total, with 49 rounds raised by 18 companies carrying $502 million, concentrated in energy and mobility businesses, where debt can finance physical assets. The first equity investor and the largest financier of those companies can be different institutions with different mandates. In the wider African technology market, debt accounted for 41 percent of recorded funding in 2025 on Partech’s method.19

Studio companies hold a similar share of each sector, but second-round rates vary widely between sectors

Sector is recorded only for companies that have raised, so sector funding rates cannot be computed and comparisons cover funded companies only. Studio companies make up 4 to 6 percent of funded African companies in most sectors of the deal record, and 7.9 percent in energy and water.

Second-round rates vary far more. Nine of the ten funded logistics companies have recorded a second round, against eight of 12 in agriculture, 23 of 37 in fintech and four of 13 in healthcare (Exhibit 20). Fintech is the largest sector, with $899 million, while Deep tech and waste management are almost absent. Moove alone accounts for $672 million of the $788 million raised in logistics, so a sector’s capital total can reflect a single company.

Funding rates are highest at West African studios, though the differences between regions could be due to chance

Portfolio entries at West African studios reach an external round at 38 percent, against 25 to 26 percent in East and Southern Africa and 17 percent in North Africa (Exhibit 21). The differences are significant when each entry is counted separately (p = 0.015), but not once entries are grouped by studio (p = 0.69), so they could be due to chance. Côte d’Ivoire records the highest rate, 54 percent, on a small base of seven of 13 entries, all from one studio’s standard pre-seed package (Chapter 4), and Nigeria follows at 37 percent. The weight of Nigeria, Kenya, South Africa and Egypt in the sample mirrors the wider market, where these four countries took 72 percent of African tech funding in 2025.20 Chapters 4 and 5 examine who writes the first external cheque and which studios attract it.
