Chapter Four

The investor pipeline is thin, fragmented and mostly non-African by Series B

In this report

The first external cheque into an African studio company comes from a fragmented investor base, and most first round investors appear in only one first round. African-headquartered investors are prominent at entry and hold a small share of participations by Series B.

African investors hold most pre-seed participations and few at Series B and later

Investors headquartered in Africa take 59 percent of named participations in pre-seed rounds, falling to 30 percent at seed, 9 percent at Series B and 4 percent at Series C and later (Exhibit 22).

A participation is one named investor in one round, and the 316 external rounds record 906 of them by 451 investors. Across all stages, North American investors account for 37.4 percent, African investors 31.1 percent, European investors 22.3 percent and others or unrecorded 9.2 percent.

The African share is close to AVCA’s estimate that African investors made up about one third of active participants in Africa’s 506 venture deals in 2025, though the two populations differ.21

Capital concentrates in the later rounds where African investors are scarce (Exhibit 19).

First cheques come from many investors, the most active with eight each

Just under half of the 451 named investors, 222, have joined a first external round, and 71 percent of them did so only once (Exhibit 23). The most active have written eight first cheques each, and the ten most frequent account for 18 percent of named first round participations. Investors headquartered in Africa take 42 percent of named first round participations, 11 percentage points above their 31.1 percent share across all rounds. Angel investors recorded without names join a further 19 first rounds and are not counted.

The first cheque market for studio companies is broad, and few large institutions take part. Few investors recur at the first round, so new investors find few established leads to follow. Companies backed by the most frequent first cheque writers have still mostly raised again. The share that went on to raise again is above the 56 percent average for funded studio companies at eight of the 12 investors in Exhibit 23, from the global accelerator Y Combinator (six of seven) to the Nigerian fund Microtraction (four of four). Nigeria is home to four of the 12, more than any other country. Each investor backed eight or fewer of these companies, so the pattern is one to test with more data.

Few of the most active investors specialise in studio companies, and they enter at different stages or through grants and debt

Studio companies are a small part of most active investors’ portfolios (Exhibit 24). Y Combinator, Techstars and Launch Africa each record between 133 and 198 deals across Africa since 2019. These investors also enter in different ways. Early-stage funds such as Kepple Africa Ventures and Future Africa come in at pre-seed or seed in at least two thirds of their studio company rounds. DEG and GIZ arrive mainly through grants, and Lendahand through debt. The two studio vehicles are the exception, with most of their recorded deals in studio companies.

Leads are disclosed for a minority of first rounds

Published announcements identify a lead, he investor that sets a round’s terms and usually commits the largest share, for only 23 of the 69 first rounds with two or more named investors (Exhibit 25).

A further 55 first rounds have a single named investor, the only disclosed candidate to have set the terms. Leads are disclosed mainly in rounds of $1 million or more, which account for 18 of the 23, while most first rounds below $1 million have a single named investor (47 of 77). African-headquartered investors hold 11 of the 30 named lead positions, and MaC Venture Capital and a corporate studio are the only investors named as lead in two first rounds.

Studio companies raise in four regional capital markets

No investor has backed five or more studio companies in more than one region, so the investor networks serving studio companies differ substantially across the four regions (Exhibit 26). Launch Africa comes closest, with six in West Africa and four in Southern Africa. North American investors account for 42 percent of named participations in West African studio company rounds, and European investors for 35 percent in East Africa. African investors hold 51 percent of participations in Southern Africa and 60 percent in North Africa, though the North African figure rests on only 20 participations. Studio vehicles appear among the frequent investors in three of the four regions. Their presence may add early capital and co-investment capacity, though the data do not establish whether they anchor rounds or reserve capital for follow-on rounds.

In East and West Africa, which hold 96 of the 132 funded entries, the first cheque market is fragmented and few investors recur across companies, which leaves little evidence of a concentrated, repeat investor network serving studio companies. Whether rounds there also lack a committed lead remains a hypothesis, because an unnamed lead is not an absent one, and founder and investor interviews are needed to test it. North Africa shows a different pattern, examined in Chapter 5.

Fewer than half of later rounds include an investor from an earlier round

Only 41.9 percent of eligible later rounds (75 of 179) include an investor from an earlier round of the same company. A later round is eligible when it and an earlier round of the same company both name an investor (Annex A.3). Repeat co-investment is concentrated in one Côte d’Ivoire studio, all seven of whose funded companies enter the record through a pre-seed round of about $800,000 with co-investors linked to its published partnership with the Ring Africa fund of Ring Capital, an independent French impact investor. Those repeat pairings appear to reflect an established partnership more than broad market.22 Elsewhere, the pattern is consistent with the white paper’s observation that limited downstream capital pushes studios into fundraising roles.23

Co-investor networks are broad but partnerships rarely repeat, while three follow-on routes recur

Among the 303 external rounds with named investors, 146 include two or more, and these rounds average three named investor participations. LoftyInc Capital and Kepple Africa Ventures have the largest recorded co-investor networks in the sample, each appearing alongside 48 distinct investors in studio company rounds (Exhibit 27). These firms are potential entry points for a new investor into the sample’s wider co-investment network.

Across all of a company’s rounds, five investor sequences recur in three companies each and form three routes (Exhibit 28). Nigerian companies move from local seed investors to US accelerators and global investors, Côte d’Ivoire companies from the studio package to European development finance, and Kenyan and Nigerian companies from development finance to impact investors. For studios, these routes indicate who is likely to write the next cheque, though whether that knowledge improves the terms of the earlier round remains an open question.
