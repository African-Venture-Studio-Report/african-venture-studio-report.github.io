Chapter Five

Studios’ own funds are the strongest fundability marker

Studios with their own funds are associated with higher funding rates

Portfolio entries at studios with their own funds reach an external round at 52 percent, against 17 percent at single entity studios, using the FMO, GIZ SAIS and Briter structural classification.24 The gap holds when studios themselves are compared, with median funding rates of 50 and 7.8 percent, and after allowing for region, where the odds are almost six times as high. This is the report’s most robust finding. The gap remains significant after correcting for multiple tests and for clustering within studios (Exhibit 40), and persists without the largest portfolio, at 45 percent (Exhibit 29).

Funded companies at studios with their own funds also raise more, with median external capital of $4.19 million against $1.05 million at single entity studios (Exhibit 30). Funding is concentrated in studios with capital of their own. The four studios with the most funded companies all have dedicated investment capital, three through internal funds and one through a partner fund, and together account for almost half of funded entries, 63 of 132.

The fund itself, company selection or the studio’s market could each explain the higher funding rate

Interview evidence leans towards selection. One studio now directs roughly 70 percent of its capital to existing businesses. Co-creating removes the hardest risk, finding someone to run the business, and fundraising is easier when the studio does not own the whole cap table. Confidential portfolio data shared for this report put companies built from scratch at roughly 2 percent, by value, of the follow-on capital raised across that portfolio. The figures include the studio’s own investment and cannot be generalised across studios, so they set a direction to test rather than a conclusion.

The deal record offers one check against the first explanation in Exhibit 31. Ten of the 27 funded companies whose first round included their own studio or its fund alongside an outside investor have raised again (37%), against 60 of the other 97 (62%, p = 0.03), although the difference does not survive correction for multiple testing (Exhibit 40). Studio co-investment may help companies secure a first round they would otherwise struggle to raise. The lower follow-on rate fits that reading, but the data cannot show whether co-investment bridges companies to outside capital or reflects weaker underlying companies.

Evidence from repeat company building outside Africa is consistent with the difference by structure without resolving its cause. In McKinsey’s 2025 survey of 715 executives, corporations that had built three or more ventures generated 1.9 times revenue per dollar invested before break-even, against 1.3 times for those that had built one or two.25 The survey measures revenue productivity in corporate venture building, not fundraising, and corporate builders differ from independent studios in capital and incentives. It suggests that repetition may matter, and in this sample the studios with the most funded companies are also those with dedicated capital.

Visibility to investors and the ease of investing and exiting also shape fundability

A studio’s own fund is not the only marker of fundability. Two conditions outside the company also bear on it, whether investors can find the studio and whether they can invest in and exit from its companies. Where either fails, a sound company may still struggle to raise.

Recorded funding rates are higher where studios are better documented, so directory-based benchmarks may overstate outcomes

The 27 studios this study adds record an external round for 13 percent of their 123 portfolio entries, against 34 percent of the 341 entries at the 41 studios in published directories (p < 0.001, Exhibit 32). Entries cluster within studios, and the difference is weaker when studios are compared. Seven of the 17 additions with at least three disclosed entries have at least one funded company, against 21 of 28 listed studios, with median funding rates of 0 and 24 percent, but only one of four studio-level tests clears the correction threshold. Neither one portfolio nor structure explains the difference. Listed studios remain at 29 percent without the largest portfolio, and among single entity studios the rates are 19 percent for listed studios and 13 percent for additions.

In North Africa, the constraint may be cross-border structure rather than the companies

North African portfolio entries have the lowest recorded funding rate of the four regions, at 17 percent. Neither of the two Tunisian studios has a recorded external round among its ten disclosed portfolio entries. The eight funded companies have raised $4.2 million of disclosed external capital between them, 0.2 percent of the capital traced into studio companies. Tunisia’s 2018 Startup Act has eased capital entry for labelled startups, which may open a foreign currency account, acquire assets abroad or take stakes in foreign companies without prior authorisation.26 Published reporting points to a further possible constraint. GoMyCode, one of Tunisia’s best known startups, moved its headquarters to the Netherlands to raise in euros from investors in Dubai, the United Kingdom and France, and its co-founder described going international for a Tunisian startup as “almost impossible”.27 The African Development Bank describes investors in Egypt asking startups seeking larger rounds to set up an offshore holding company in a jurisdiction offering “better protections and easier transactions”.28 AVCA data put the share of venture deals between 2014 and 2019 that went to African startups headquartered outside Africa at 21 percent.29 The AVCA figure shows that offshore incorporation is common across the continent, but it does not by itself explain North African funding rates.

If offshore holding structures and clear exit routes are prerequisites for some investors, locally incorporated companies may face added barriers to larger rounds regardless of quality, which would point towards examining cross-border investment and exit rules alongside early-stage support. This remains a hypothesis, resting on 47 entries, eight funded companies and published reporting from Tunisia and Egypt, and no North African studio, founder or investor has yet been interviewed.

Studio stakes vary widely and are not recorded

The FMO, GIZ SAIS and Briter white paper reports studio stakes from 20 percent to more than 50 percent, with venture-oriented studios generally below 30 percent. The Global Startup Studio Network separately reports an average studio stake of 34 percent, from a different population and method. The most transparent African example, a Côte d’Ivoire studio, publishes terms of 25 percent for an in-kind build valued at €500,000 and a further 12.5 percent for €250,000 in cash from its partner co-investment vehicle. Both published sources give a combined pre-seed stake of 32.5 percent, which the two terms do not reproduce, so the combined stake remains unconfirmed.30

Studio ownership can also affect how outside investors assess the management team and founder incentives. In a survey of 885 institutional venture capitalists, the management team is most often cited as the most important factor, ahead of business model and market.31 Where a studio holds more equity than the founding team, outside investors may question how control and founder incentives are structured, and interview evidence describes a studio moving towards existing businesses partly because fundraising is easier when it does not own the whole cap table. No dataset records studio stakes, so their effect on funding rates cannot yet be tested.

Investors and DFIs can weigh a studio’s own capital now, and ask for the stakes and build routes no dataset records

A studio’s own capital is the marker most consistently associated with reaching an external round, and a signal for investors and development finance institutions to track while evidence on its cause builds (Exhibit 33). Build route and studio stake, which could explain much of the difference it marks, are not yet recorded anywhere, so investors can close the gap at diligence by asking studios for both. The markers support diagnosis rather than prediction, since no weights are estimated and none is shown to cause a company to raise.

A studio’s own capital matters at the first external round and again at the second. It marks which studios’ companies reach a first external round, and Chapter 6 shows that the follow-on advantage sits there too, with 69 percent of funded companies at studios with their own funds raising again, against 28 percent of other startups. That pattern supports the white paper’s central finding once companies have raised.
