Chapter Six

The studio advantage appears after the first external round

Studio companies that secure a first external round go on to raise again more often than other startups, most clearly at studios with their own funds. Once companies have raised, the African record supports the central finding of the FMO, GIZ SAIS and Briter white paper, and its observation that studio companies raise larger first rounds. Other published findings, several drawn from international benchmarks and practitioner materials, cannot yet be confirmed on African data, and none is contradicted by it.

The second round is where the studio advantage shows

Funded studio companies raise a second round within 24 months at 48 percent, against 28 percent for comparable startups, and after allowing for region and first round year their odds of doing so are 2.21 times as high (Chapter 3). The second round is where early external capital either compounds or stalls. Across the comparison cohort, 64 percent of studio companies have recorded no round since September 2024, against 84 percent of other startups. A higher follow-on rate means more companies reach the scale at which exits and share sales recycle capital into new companies (Chapter 1).

Two explanations fit the advantage, and the data do not yet separate them. The first is capital to follow on. Companies of studios with their own funds raise again at 69 percent, against 29 percent at single entity studios, the rate of other startups, and one reading is that the capacity to keep backing a company matters as much as building it. The second is selection, since studios filter ideas before any investor sees them, and studios with funds also back companies they did not build. The result also leans on the largest portfolio. Without it, the studio rate falls to 39 percent and the difference is no longer statistically significant, so the pattern holds in the full sample while its cause remains open (Chapter 9).

A funded studio company offers investors a stronger record of reaching its next round than other funded startups, most clearly where the studio can follow on. Studios and their funders see the model’s value most clearly after the first external cheque, which makes reaching that cheque, and holding capital to support companies beyond it, the priorities Chapter 8 addresses.

Most published findings await larger African cohorts and new data to be tested locally

Exhibit 34 sets seven published findings against the deal record, with studio companies and other startups drawn from the same source so that definitions, thresholds and dates match. Two are supported, two are not reproduced on cohorts too small to settle them, and three cannot be tested directly with the data available.

The international Series A benchmark describes a different market

Across the four definitions tested, the African studio rate ranges from 7 to 17 percent, with no statistically significant difference from other startups. The 72 percent figure is the most widely cited statistic in the studio literature. Its source does not publish a sample size or cohort window and draws on studios that chose to report, so it describes a different population from African portfolios.32 The African range rests on four studio companies reaching Series A under every definition, so it cannot rule out an advantage either. African studios are therefore better underwritten on African evidence.

Exits are too rare to compare, and the gap in female chief executives is not significant

Recorded acquisitions are too few to establish a difference in exit performance, with three funded studio companies recording one, even as venture-backed exits across the wider market reached a record 34 in 2025.33 Founder access cannot be tested, because no dataset records the gender of founders entering studios. On the nearest available measure, female chief executives lead 12.9 percent of funded studio companies, against 16.7 percent of other funded startups (p = 0.32). Because a studio selects and recruits its founders, it shapes who leads its companies more directly than an external investor does, so the 3.8 percentage point gap is relevant even though it is not statistically significant. The data do not show whether that control widens or narrows access for underrepresented founders. Female-founded startups took 19 percent of African equity deals and 10 percent of equity funding in 2025, a market-wide measure based on founder gender that is not directly comparable with these results.34

The case for studios is strongest after the first external cheque

Studio companies that raise a first external round show a follow-on advantage, concentrated at studios with their own funds, and their first recorded rounds are larger. Faster funding, a higher Series A rate, more exits, wider founder access and sectoral de-risking remain to be tested on larger African cohorts and new data, and no significant result contradicts any of them. The studio model therefore shows its value most clearly after the first external cheque. Chapter 7 examines why so many companies stall before it.
