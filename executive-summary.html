Nearly three in four companies in disclosed African venture studio portfolios have no recorded external round. The gap to the published benchmark sits at that first step, and the advantage over other startups appears only once companies have raised, concentrated in studios with their own funds. The first external cheque is therefore where most studio companies stop.

What the report studies

Briter’s 2026 white paper, produced with FMO and GIZ SAIS, and its summit directory document 48 African venture studios and form the most comprehensive public record of the sector. This report extends that landscape to 68 verified studios, 27 of them not yet in Briter’s publications. Forty-nine disclose portfolios, which together hold 456 companies, matched to Africa: The Big Deal records from January 2019 to September 2026.

Two qualifications apply. First, portfolios do not separate companies that studios built, co-created or invested in, so “studio companies” covers all three. Second, the sample is geographically concentrated. Nigeria, South Africa and Kenya host 40 of the 68 studios, and Central Africa is represented by one studio in Angola, grouped with Southern Africa. Interview evidence is limited to studio practitioners, with founders and external investors not yet interviewed. Chapter 2 sets out the method and sample construction, and Exhibit 42 shows how each limitation moves the findings.

Key research findings

Statistical detail sits in the chapters cited, and Exhibit 41 grades the strength of evidence behind each headline figure.

01

Most of the gap in funding rates sits at the first external round

Africa: The Big Deal records an external round for 124 of the 456 companies in disclosed studio portfolios, a funding rate of 27%. Briter’s white paper reports 40% or 49%, depending on the edition, for companies studios built in-house, while this figure covers every company a studio lists, including those it co-created or invested in. The difference at the first round is 13 percentage points against the 40% figure and 22 percentage points against the 49% figure, and it narrows at each later round, which suggests the gap sits mainly among companies yet to raise. Small rounds missing from the database may understate our rate, and failed ventures removed from studio websites may overstate it, so the net effect is unknown.

Chapter 3 · Exhibits 11 and 13

02

Follow-on funding rates are higher, but concentrated in studios with their own funds

Studio companies first funded from January 2021 to September 2024 raised a second round within 24 months at 48% (80 companies), against 28% for other startups (1,612). The advantage sits with studios that have their own funds, whose companies raised again at 69%, while companies of single entity studios, at 29%, are in line with other startups. The difference is statistically significant on the standard test, even allowing for clustering within studios, but it depends partly on the largest portfolio, without which the studio rate falls to 39%, and it does not clear the stricter threshold applied when many comparisons are tested together.

Chapter 3 · Exhibits 14 and 15

03

Investor participation is fragmented, and African investors fall away at later stages

Just under half of the 451 named investors (222) joined at least one first external round. Most of them (71%) did so only once, and none joined more than eight first rounds. African investors account for 59% of named investor participations at pre-seed but 4% at Series C and later, and fewer than half of later rounds with named investors (42%) include one from an earlier round. Later rounds therefore rely largely on investors from outside Africa, for reasons the deal record cannot yet establish.

Chapter 4 · Exhibits 22 and 23

04

Portfolio companies at studios with their own funds reach a first external round three times as often

Across all portfolio companies, 52% at studios with their own funds have reached a first external round, against 17% at single entity studios. Finding 02 shows the same pattern at follow-on, so studios’ own funds are linked to an advantage at both entry and follow-on. It remains an association, since portfolios mix built and backed companies and studios with funds may select stronger businesses from the outset.

Chapter 5 · Exhibits 29 and 30

05

More visible studios have higher recorded funding rates

Portfolio companies at the 41 studios already listed in published industry directories reach an external round at 34%, against 13% at the 27 studios added by this study, though the difference is less certain when the comparison is made between studios. Funding and visibility may reinforce each other, as funded studios are likely to feature more often in publications and at industry events. Benchmarks built from public sources may therefore lean towards better funded studios, a common feature of how an emerging asset class becomes documented.

Chapter 5 · Exhibit 32

06

International studio benchmarks cannot yet be confirmed on African data

The widely cited figure that 72% of seed funded studio companies reach Series A comes from international studio data. In Africa, fewer than one in five seed funded studio companies reach Series A under any definition of a seed round, in line with other startups. Recorded exits are 2.4% for funded studio companies against 2.9% for other funded startups, and women lead 13% of funded studio companies against 17% of others. No difference is statistically significant. Four studio companies have reached Series A and three have exited, too few to confirm or rule out an advantage. The African case for studios rests on the follow-on advantage in finding 02.

Strategic implications for decision makers

The eight actions below fall to four groups of decision makers (Chapter 8). The most important are that studios publish funding outcomes for every company they hold, DFIs and other LPs backing studios concentrate support on the first external round, where the gap sits, and venture investors treat studio portfolios as a regular source of first deals.

  1. 1
    Funders, DFIs and LPs

    Require studios to report the share of all portfolio companies that reach a first external round, alongside follow-on rates.

  2. 2
    Funders, DFIs and LPs

    Pilot co-investment at the first external round alongside a committed independent investor, and scale it only if first round rates rise.

  3. 3
    Venture studio founders

    Design each company’s route to its first external investor before building it, whether through an internal fund, a committed partner vehicle or an agreed syndicate.

  4. 4
    Venture studio founders

    Publish standard equity terms, including a ceiling on the studio’s stake that falls as founders take over. Investors weigh founder ownership closely, though the data cannot yet show whether a ceiling lifts first round rates.

  5. 5
    Institutional and venture investors

    Build repeat relationships with studios as a source of first deals. Most investors in studio companies’ first rounds (71%) have joined only one, so few have yet built a pipeline of first deals from studio portfolios.

  6. 6
    Institutional and venture investors

    Assess the studio’s ability to fund later rounds as closely as the company itself. The second-round advantage sits with studios that have their own funds, while companies of studios without a fund raise again at about the same rate as startups outside studios (29% against 28%).

  7. 7
    Industry bodies and regional policymakers

    Establish a common disclosure standard with studios, DFIs and LPs, covering every portfolio company’s launch year, build route and status, updated each year. Published funding rates for studios would then cover every company they hold, including those at less visible studios that public sources capture less well.

  8. 8
    Industry bodies and regional policymakers

    Assess whether cross-border capital and exit rules are holding back fundraising in North Africa, including Egypt, one of the continent’s four largest venture markets. North African studio portfolios have the lowest recorded funding rate of the four regions, at 17%, and published reporting from Egypt and Tunisia describes startups moving their holding companies abroad to raise larger rounds.

The headline rate is robust, and what drives differences between studios remains open

The headline funding rate holds under every definition tested, but what drives the differences between studios remains open. The share of portfolio companies with a recorded external round stays between 26 and 29 percent. The follow-on advantage holds across regions, years, sectors and stages, but leans on the largest portfolio and could still reflect chance. Studios’ own funds show the firmest link to higher funding rates, though it is not yet clear whether these studios build stronger companies or back them. Higher rates at studios listed in industry directories are less certain when studios are compared directly. Findings on how studio support ends rest on limited practitioner interviews, and the view that cross-border rules hold back North African fundraising rests on published reporting.

The evidence at a glance

Studio companies trail at the first external round, then raise again more often than other startups.
